Switches and truth

Dr Farhad Babaee

Nothing on this page is numbered. The items here are not taken from a set of lecture notes, and a number that does not match the notes you are reading from is worse than no number at all.

A switch is a statement

In 1937 Claude Shannon noticed that the algebra of switching circuits and the algebra of true and false are the same algebra. The dictionary is short enough to give in one line. A switch is a statement. Closed means true and open means false, and the lamp at the end of the circuit lights exactly when the whole expression is true.

That gives you three things to build with.

  • $P \wedge Q$ is two switches in series. Current has to get through both, so the lamp lights only when both are closed.
  • $P \vee Q$ is two switches in parallel. Either branch will do, so the lamp lights when at least one is closed.
  • $\neg P$ is a break contact. This is the piece people usually mean when they ask what negation looks like in a circuit: a contact wired so that it conducts when the switch is not pressed. Electricians call it normally closed; in logic gates the same idea is the inverter, drawn as a triangle with a small bubble on its output. The bubble is the negation.

Throw the switches and watch the three lamps. The marked contact in the third circuit is the break contact: it conducts when $P$ is off.

Food for thought

There is no switch for $\Rightarrow$. Implication has to be built out of the other three, and the truth table says how: $P \Rightarrow Q$ is the same statement as $\neg P \vee Q$. So the circuit for an implication is a break contact for $P$ in parallel with an ordinary contact for $Q$. Watch for it below.

Are two statements the same?

Two statements are logically equivalent when they are true in exactly the same circumstances, which is to say that their truth tables agree row for row. In circuits that reads even more plainly: wire both up to the same switches, and ask whether the two lamps ever disagree.

So here is a machine for settling it. Pick a pair of statements, and the checker walks through every combination of the switches in turn. If it ever finds a combination where one lamp is on and the other is off, it stops there and reports that the two are not equivalent, with the combination that separates them. If it gets through all of them with the lamps always agreeing, they are equivalent.

Three of the pairs in that list are worth sitting with. The contrapositive $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ agrees with $P \Rightarrow Q$ on every row, which is why you are allowed to prove either one in place of the other. The converse-negation $\neg P \Rightarrow \neg Q$ does not, and the checker stops on the row that separates them. And De Morgan's laws turn a series pair into a parallel pair the moment you negate it, which is exactly what the two circuits show.

Food for thought

The checker stops at the first disagreement, so a verdict of not equivalent arrives after one row, while equivalent needs all of them. That asymmetry is not an accident of the program. Refuting a universal claim takes one counterexample; establishing it takes every case. With two switches there are four cases, with three there are eight, and with $n$ there are $2^{n}$, which is why checking cases stops being a proof strategy almost immediately.
